# Copywriting Craft & Persuasion Techniques

## Core Principle

Great travel copy does not describe. It activates.
It moves a person from passive reading to physical desire — the kind that ends with a booking.
Every technique in this file serves that single purpose.

Apply these automatically. They are not optional extras — they are the craft.

---

## Structural Frameworks

Use these to architect any piece of copy before writing a word.

### AIDA — The Classic Conversion Arc
For: listing descriptions, landing pages, email campaigns, ad copy

- **A — Attention:** Stop the scroll. The opening must earn the next sentence.
- **I — Interest:** Draw them in with specificity. A detail that feels true and observed.
- **D — Desire:** Make them feel what it would be like to be there. The guest as hero.
- **A — Action:** One clear, frictionless next step. Never two.

### PAS — Problem, Agitate, Solution
For: re-engagement emails, off-season promotions, win-back campaigns

- **P — Problem:** Name the feeling they have right now (tired, overworked, disconnected)
- **A — Agitate:** Deepen it. Make them feel it more acutely.
- **S — Solution:** Position the property as the answer. Specific, not generic.

### The Desire Bridge
For: social captions, ad copy, email subject lines

Open with where they are now → bridge to where the property takes them.
"You are in [current reality]. Here, you become [desired state]."
Never name the gap explicitly — let the reader feel it themselves.

### The Rule of One
For: every single piece of copy, always

One reader. One idea. One action.
If a piece of copy is trying to say three things, it is saying nothing.
Before writing — identify the single most important thing this copy must make someone feel or do.
Everything else is in service of that one thing, or it is cut.

## Sentence-Level Techniques

### The "So What?" Filter
Every feature must be pushed through this filter until it becomes a benefit — then an emotion.

Example:
- Feature: "Private infinity pool" → So what?
- Benefit: "You swim without an audience" → So what?
- Emotion: "For the first time in months, you are completely alone with the horizon"

Never stop at the feature. Never stop at the benefit. Reach the emotion.

### Open Loops
The human brain cannot leave a loop open. Use this.

Start a sentence or paragraph in a way that creates an unanswered question in the reader's mind.
They will keep reading to close it.

Example: "Most guests say the same thing when they arrive. Not about the view — about the quiet."
The reader has to know what they say. The loop is open. They read on.

### The Unexpected Specific
Vague copy is invisible. Specific detail creates credibility and stops the eye.

Not: "beautifully designed interiors"
But: "hand-thrown ceramic bowls from a workshop twenty minutes down the coast"

The specific detail does not need to be dramatic. It needs to feel true and observed.
Specificity is the fastest way to earn trust.

### Sentence Rhythm & Pacing
Rhythm is felt before it is understood. Vary it deliberately.

Short sentences hit hard.
Medium sentences carry the reader forward at a comfortable pace and build momentum.
Longer sentences — the kind that move through several images or ideas before they arrive at their conclusion — are best used when you want the reader to slow down and stay inside a moment.

Then stop. One word. Land.

Read every paragraph aloud before delivering. If you stumble, the reader stumbles.

### The False Floor
Make the copy feel like it is ending — then deliver one more line that reframes everything.

Used at the end of listing descriptions, emails, and blog posts.
The reader thinks they have arrived. Then one final sentence opens a new door.

Example: "Centro House sleeps six. But the memories it makes tend to last considerably longer."

## Emotional Triggers

### Sensory Activation
Luxury travel copy must engage all five senses — not just sight.

- **Sight:** The light, the colour, the line of the horizon
- **Sound:** The silence, the cicadas, the water
- **Touch:** The weight of the linen, the warmth of the stone, the coolness of the pool
- **Smell:** Salt air, wild thyme, espresso at 7am
- **Taste:** Where relevant — the fig from the garden, the local wine at dusk

Most copywriters only write what can be photographed. Write what cannot.

### The Nostalgia Paradox
Luxury travel sells a future experience — but the most powerful emotional trigger is memory.
Write copy that makes the reader remember a feeling they have not had yet.
"The kind of morning you forget exists until you're in the middle of one."

### Identity Alignment
People do not book properties. They book versions of themselves.
Write to who the guest wants to be in this place — not who they are at their desk.
The design-conscious traveller. The person who knows where to go before anyone else does.
The couple who still knows how to be alone together.

### Earned Exclusivity
Never say "exclusive." Create the feeling of exclusivity through scarcity of language itself.
Sparse copy feels more expensive than verbose copy.
What you do not say is as powerful as what you do.
A property description that trusts the reader to fill in the gaps feels premium.
One that over-explains feels insecure.

## Persuasion Mechanics

### Social Proof Positioning
Where you place social proof matters as much as what you say.

- **Early in copy:** Establishes credibility before the reader has formed a judgment
- **Mid-copy:** Reinforces a claim you have just made ("Guests consistently tell us...")
- **End of copy:** Closes doubt at the moment of decision

Never lead with an award or review. Lead with the experience. Let proof arrive as confirmation.

### Scarcity & Urgency — The Luxury Version
For mass-market travel: "Only 2 rooms left!" works.
For premium and luxury: it cheapens. Use a different register entirely.

Instead of manufactured urgency, use:
- **Calendar scarcity:** "September is the last month the island belongs to itself."
- **Experiential scarcity:** "There is only one caldera. There are very few places to watch it like this."
- **Seasonal truth:** "The light in October is something photographers come back for specifically."

Urgency that is true is always more powerful than urgency that is manufactured.

### The Soft CTA
For luxury properties, hard CTAs ("Book Now!") signal desperation.
Use CTAs that feel like an invitation, not a sales push.

Strong soft CTAs:
- "Reserve your dates"
- "Begin planning your stay"
- "Check availability"
- "Enquire about [month]"
- "This is where you start"

The softer the CTA, the more confident the brand appears.
Confidence is the most powerful conversion tool in luxury.

### Objection Pre-emption
Identify the most likely reason someone would hesitate — then dissolve it inside the copy
before it forms as a conscious doubt.

Common hesitations for luxury STRs:
- "Is it worth the price?" → Dissolve with specificity about what is included and irreplaceable
- "Will it look like the photos?" → Dissolve with specific, verifiable details
- "Is it right for our group?" → Dissolve with a clear picture of who thrives here

Never address objections defensively. Fold the answer into the narrative.

## Editing Principles

### Kill the Throat-Clearing
The first sentence of most first drafts is the writer warming up, not the reader being served.
Delete the first sentence. Read again. Often the real opening was always the second.

### The Adjective Audit
After writing, scan for every adjective. Ask: does this earn its place, or is it a placeholder?
If you cannot replace it with a specific detail that shows the same thing — it is a placeholder. Cut it.

### Active vs. Passive Voice
Active voice moves. Passive voice stalls.
Not: "Guests are welcomed by the views" — But: "The views stop you at the door."

### Read Aloud Test
If a sentence cannot be read aloud naturally in one breath — restructure it.
If a paragraph read aloud has no rhythm variation — rewrite it.
Copy is heard before it is understood, even when read silently.

### The Last Line Rule
The last line of any piece of copy is the one that closes the sale or closes the door.
It should be the sharpest line in the piece.
Never end on logistics, amenities, or administration.
End on the feeling. End on the image. End on the open door.
