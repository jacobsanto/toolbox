# CanvasGPT Reference — Logo design, brand systems, and asset specifications

## Brand Identity vs Visual Identity

**Brand identity:** Everything that makes a brand recognisable and distinctive —
personality, values, voice, and visual expression together.

**Visual identity:** The visual component of brand identity —
logo, colours, typography, imagery, layout principles.

CanvasGPT works with visual identity. The visual system must serve
the brand identity — not define it.

Before designing any visual system, the brand's personality, values, and
positioning must be understood. Visual decisions without strategic foundation
produce aesthetics without meaning.

## Logo Design Principles

### What a Logo Must Do
1. **Identify** — immediately communicate who this is
2. **Differentiate** — stand apart from the competitive landscape
3. **Endure** — work for 10+ years without feeling dated
4. **Scale** — work at 16px favicon and 10m billboard simultaneously
5. **Reproduce** — work in black and white, one colour, reversed

### Logo Types

**Wordmark (logotype)**
The brand name as the logo. Typography-based.
Use for: Brands where the name is short, distinctive, or itself the asset.
Examples: Google, FedEx, Coca-Cola
Requires: Distinctive, custom (or custom-modified) typography.

**Lettermark (monogram)**
Initials as the logo.
Use for: Long brand names needing a compact mark. Established brands.
Examples: IBM, HP, CNN
Risk: Requires brand awareness to be meaningful.

**Pictorial mark (icon/symbol)**
A recognisable image or icon.
Use for: Global brands where language independence matters.
Examples: Apple, Twitter/X, Nike
Risk: Requires significant brand investment for the icon to carry meaning alone.

**Abstract mark**
A geometric or abstract form — not a recognisable image.
Use for: Brands wanting unique, ownable visual property.
Examples: Pepsi, Adidas, Chase
Risk: Requires consistent application to build recognition.

**Combination mark**
Wordmark + pictorial or abstract mark.
Use for: Most brands — especially newer ones building recognition.
Both elements can be used independently as the brand matures.
This is the most versatile and recommended approach for most brands.

**Emblem**
Text within a symbol (badge/shield).
Use for: Traditional, heritage, institution brands.
Examples: Starbucks (pre-2011), Harley-Davidson, most sports teams
Risk: Complex — difficult to scale down. Rarely appropriate for digital-first brands.

### Logo Design Rules

**Simplicity:** The most enduring logos are the simplest.
Every added element must earn its place or be removed.
The test: Can it be drawn from memory? If no — probably too complex.

**Scalability test:**
- Works at 16px × 16px (favicon)
- Works at 24px × 24px (app icon)
- Works at 200px × 200px (email header)
- Works at 1000px × 1000px (print)

**Colour independence:**
- Works in full colour
- Works in single colour (black)
- Works reversed (white on dark)
- Works in greyscale

**Space requirement:**
Define a minimum clear space around the logo (typically 1× the x-height of the wordmark).
Document this in brand guidelines. Enforce it.

## Brand Colour System

### Minimum Viable Colour System

For a new brand, this is the minimum:

| Role | Count | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | 1 | The brand's dominant colour |
| Secondary | 1 | Accent and support |
| Neutral light | 1-2 | Backgrounds, surfaces |
| Neutral dark | 1-2 | Text, dark surfaces |
| Semantic | 3 | Success, warning, error |

Total: 8-10 colours. More than this for a new brand is premature.

### Full Brand Colour System

For established brands needing a complete system:

**Primary palette:** 1 brand colour × 9 shades (50, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600, 700, 800, 900)
**Secondary palette:** 1-2 additional colours × 9 shades
**Neutral palette:** Grey scale × 9 shades
**Semantic palette:** Success, warning, error, info × 3-5 shades each

## Brand Asset Specifications

### Logo File Formats

| Format | Use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| SVG | Web, digital — preferred | Infinitely scalable, small file |
| PNG (transparent) | Digital, social media | Provide at 2× and 3× for retina |
| PDF | Print | Vector, colour-accurate |
| EPS | Legacy print/agency handoff | Vector format |
| JPG | Backgrounds only | Never for logos — no transparency |

### Required Logo Variants

Every brand should have:
1. Full colour (on white background)
2. Full colour (on brand colour background)
3. Single colour black
4. Single colour white (reversed)
5. Favicon version (simplified if needed for 16×16px)

### Naming Convention
```
[brand]-logo-[variant]-[size].[format]

Examples:
brand-logo-primary-2x.svg
brand-logo-reversed-1x.png
brand-logo-black.pdf
brand-favicon.svg
```

## Brand Guidelines Document Structure

A complete brand guidelines document covers:

1. **Brand story** — why the brand exists, what it stands for
2. **Logo** — usage rules, clear space, minimum size, incorrect usage examples
3. **Colour system** — palette, usage rules, accessibility requirements
4. **Typography** — type system, hierarchy, usage examples
5. **Imagery style** — photography direction, illustration style, what to avoid
6. **Iconography** — icon style, grid, usage rules
7. **Layout principles** — grid, spacing, composition
8. **Voice and tone** (brief reference — full version in brand identity)
9. **Application examples** — real examples across key touchpoints
10. **File assets** — where to access brand files

### The "Do / Do Not" Principle
Every brand guidelines section should show correct usage AND incorrect usage.
Showing what to avoid is as important as showing what to do.
Theoretical rules without visual examples are rarely followed consistently.

## Design Audit — Brand Identity Specific

When auditing an existing brand identity:

**Logo audit:**
- Does it work at all required sizes?
- Does it work in black and white?
- Is it distinctive in the competitive landscape?
- Has it dated significantly?

**Colour audit:**
- Are all colour combinations accessible?
- Is the system consistently applied?
- Are there too many colours being used?

**Typography audit:**
- Are more than 2 typefaces in use?
- Is there consistent hierarchy?
- Are web fonts loading correctly?

**Consistency audit:**
- Does the brand look the same across all touchpoints?
- Are there rogue colours, fonts, or styles appearing?
- Do different team members apply the brand differently?

**Refresh vs rebrand decision:**
Refresh: Same strategic positioning, evolved visual expression. Usually the right choice.
Rebrand: Changed strategic positioning, new visual expression. Only when strategy changes.
Never rebrand for aesthetic boredom. Fix the execution, not the strategy.
